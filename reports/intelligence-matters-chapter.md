## Finding

Add a separate chapter on intelligence matters. The source trail is strong
enough to justify a chapter rather than a subsection: presidential intelligence
consumption, covert action procedures, congressional notification, and
intelligence budget management all tie directly to organization and management.

## Presidential Intelligence Consumption

Add this strand to the chapter. Bush was not merely a recipient of finished
intelligence; he managed the PDB as an interactive daily process. Public CIA
source anchors indicate that he wanted working-level CIA officers to brief him,
kept the PDB as the first order of business, read it in the presence of the CIA
briefer and usually Scowcroft or Gates, used the session to ask for more
information, and restricted distribution so the Intelligence Community would
feel able to include sensitive material.

This is a Volume II subject because it shows management of intelligence at the
presidential level: who briefed, who sat in, how the document was controlled,
how follow-up questions moved back to CIA, and how intelligence entered the
morning foreign-policy workflow. It also complements the covert-action and
budget lanes by showing Bush's operating assumptions as the only former DCI to
serve as President.

Selection should avoid using a published CIA history as the selected document
unless no better records can be released. The document pull should instead look
for PDB process files: briefer debrief notes, questions/tasking logs, control
sheets, distribution instructions, Daily Diary entries, Scowcroft/Gates/Sununu
morning-meeting records, and any releasable PDB cover pages or issue-specific
supplements.

The Presidential Daily Diary and Daily Backup series, NAID 186322, should be
used as the chronology spine for this strand, as reflected in the first table on
this page. It can confirm the timing, location, participants, telephone-call
status, and surrounding meetings for intelligence and national security
briefings. It should not be treated as a substantive account of call content or
meeting minutes; pair diary references with released memcons, telcons, PDB
process records, NSC meeting files, or WHPO caption records.

## Covert Action and Congress

NSD 79 is the strongest recovered source. It rescinds NSDD 286 and conforms
covert-action procedures to the FY 1991 Intelligence Authorization Act. It
requires a presidential Finding before covert action, permits oral
authorization only in rare immediate cases with a written Finding within 48
hours, bars retroactive authorization, and requires Memoranda of Notification
for substantial changes in means, resources, operational conditions, country
scope, or risk.

The congressional-oversight section is especially important for this volume. It
requires notification to Congress for covert action and significant changes,
normally through the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence and House
Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence before initiation. In extraordinary
circumstances, the President may restrict access to congressional leadership and
the intelligence committee chairs/ranking members. If notification is delayed,
NSD 79 sets a two-working-day default and requires written memorialization and
NSC reevaluation at least every 10 days for longer delays.

## Intelligence Budget and Community Management

The budget lane should not be folded into the general Function 150 chapter.
Intelligence budgeting has a separate statutory and executive-process record.
NSD 79 itself assigns OMB responsibility for ensuring that the President's
budget provides resources consistent with approved covert-action Findings for
the congressional budget request.

The broader intelligence-budget track runs through NSR 29, NSD 67, the March
1992 NSC packets, the April 1992 Fitzwater statement, and the FY 1992-FY 1993
intelligence authorization acts. The recovered March 25, 1992 NSC files show
that the intelligence capabilities review went to the President and NSC before
Gates's planned congressional presentation. The public Fitzwater statement says
the President approved organizational changes for the intelligence community
and that the changes included significant FY 1993-FY 1997 National Foreign
Intelligence Program resource reallocations plus a new budgetary mechanism for
NFIP agencies to support multi-year funding and congressional oversight.
